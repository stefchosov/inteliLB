\begin{abstract}
Modern distributed applications are deployed across geographically dispersed
compute resources, where load balancers determine how client requests are
routed. Traditional strategies such as round-robin or lowest-latency routing
are uninformed about backend compute state, which can lead to suboptimal
tail latency and throughput when backends are heterogeneous or asymmetrically
loaded. This paper presents \textbf{inteliLB}, an HTTP load balancer
implemented in Go that supports six routing algorithms --- round-robin,
least-connections, lowest-latency, lowest-CPU, intelligent (static
multi-metric weighted scoring), and adaptive (dynamically reweighted
multi-metric scoring) --- and evaluates them against asymmetric Azure VM
backends across three geographic regions. We ran five closed-loop workload profiles
(CPU-only, high-connection, I/O-bound, bimodal, and realistic mixed) and an
open-loop saturation ramp, using 120-second windows per step to ensure
steady-state measurements. Our results show that
\textbf{least-connections outperforms all algorithms} on CPU-bound and mixed
workloads, reducing p99 tail latency by up to 55\% relative to round-robin
while matching or exceeding its throughput. Lowest-latency routing correctly
implements pure network-proximity selection, routing all traffic exclusively
to the closest backend (backend-2, northcentralus), making it optimal for
I/O-bound workloads (highest throughput at 374.7\,req/s) but prone to
concentrating CPU-bound traffic on a single node, causing early saturation
at 75\,req/s. The intelligent algorithm sustains clean operation to 150\,req/s,
matching round-robin's saturation ceiling at 200\,req/s. We discuss the
design, the closed-loop vs.\ open-loop benchmarking methodology, and
directions for future work.

\section{Problem Statement}

Load balancers are a foundational component of distributed systems, responsible
for distributing client requests across a pool of backend servers. In modern
cloud deployments, those backends are rarely homogeneous: different VM sizes,
different geographic regions, and different instantaneous load levels mean that
a routing decision that ignores compute state will systematically over-utilize
some backends while under-utilizing others.

The standard textbook approaches handle this poorly. Round-robin distributes
requests uniformly regardless of backend capacity or health. Lowest-latency
routing minimizes network round-trip time but can conflate network proximity with
compute availability --- a backend that is geographically close may be heavily
loaded. Single-metric policies (lowest CPU, least connections) capture one
dimension of backend state but miss the others.

The motivating scenario for this project is a client with low network latency
to \textbf{Region A} running at 90\% CPU utilization, and moderate latency to
\textbf{Region B} with ample available compute. A latency-based router sends
the request to Region A, where it queues behind saturated workers. An
intelligent router that weighs both network latency and compute availability
may route to Region B, accepting a small RTT penalty in exchange for a large
reduction in queueing delay, yielding lower overall response time.

\textbf{Research question:} Do multi-metric routing strategies (intelligent,
adaptive) meaningfully outperform single-metric and round-robin baselines
when backends are asymmetric in compute capacity and geographic distance,
under a range of workload types and offered loads?

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Multi-metric intelligent routing will reduce tail latency
and improve throughput stability over single-metric and round-robin approaches,
particularly under CPU-heavy asymmetric load.

\section{Solution Description}

\subsection{System Architecture}

inteliLB is a pure-Go HTTP reverse proxy that sits between a client and a pool
of backend servers. The load balancer runs locally; backends are remote Azure
VMs. The overall data path is:

\begin{center}
\texttt{Client $\longrightarrow$ inteliLB (local) $\longrightarrow$
Backend-N (Azure, cross-region)}
\end{center}

The balancer exposes two endpoints to the client: \texttt{/work} (proxied to a
backend) and \texttt{/lb/stats} (JSON snapshot of per-backend metrics and
request counts). A hot-swap REST endpoint (\texttt{POST /lb/algorithm}) allows
the active routing algorithm to be changed without restarting the process.

\subsection{Backend Infrastructure}

Three Azure VMs were deployed across distinct geographic regions to produce
heterogeneous network latency and compute capacity. TCP connect-time RTT was
measured from the load balancer host to each backend on port 22 (ICMP is
blocked by Azure NSGs):

\centering
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\toprule
Backend & Region & VM SKU & Effective vCPUs & TCP RTT \\
\midrule
backend-1 & westus2 (Washington)    & Standard\_D2s\_v3 & 1 (taskset pinned) & $\approx$48\,ms \\
backend-2 & northcentralus (Illinois) & Standard\_D2s\_v3 & 2               & $\approx$14\,ms \\
backend-3 & eastus2 (Virginia)      & Standard\_D4s\_v3 & 4                  & $\approx$39\,ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Azure backend layout. backend-2 (northcentralus) is the closest to
the test client at $\approx$14\,ms RTT; backend-3 is the most powerful with
4 effective vCPUs. backend-1 is the most distant \emph{and} the weakest.}
\end{table}

Each backend runs a Go HTTP server (\texttt{cmd/backend}) that handles two
request types:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CPU work:} $\textit{intensity} \times 50{,}000$ rounds of
        SHA-256 hashing, divided evenly across all available vCPUs via
        \texttt{runtime.NumCPU()} goroutines. Total work per request is
        constant; a 4-vCPU backend finishes in one-quarter the wall time of
        a 1-vCPU backend.
  \item \textbf{I/O work:} A fixed 250\,ms \texttt{time.Sleep}, modelling
        network or disk I/O.
\end{itemize}

Backends also expose \texttt{/metrics} (CPU\%, memory\%, active connections)
and \texttt{/health} endpoints that the load balancer polls on configurable
intervals (default: 5\,s).

\subsection{Routing Algorithms}

\paragraph{Round-Robin.}
A simple atomic counter cycles through healthy backends. No metric data
required. Serves as the primary baseline.

\paragraph{Least-Connections.}
Selects the backend with the fewest in-flight requests, tracked via an atomic
\texttt{int64} counter that is incremented on request start and decremented on
completion.

\paragraph{Lowest-Latency.}
Selects the backend with the lowest observed network RTT. The balancer tracks
a per-backend \emph{baseline RTT} (\texttt{BaselineRTTMs}) --- the minimum
round-trip time observed to each backend's \texttt{/metrics} endpoint ---
and routes every request to the backend with the smallest value. This is a
pure network-proximity algorithm: it consistently favours the geographically
closest backend regardless of its compute load. It is well-suited to I/O-bound
workloads where network round-trip dominates response time, and serves as a
clean geographic-routing baseline for comparison against compute-aware strategies.

\paragraph{Lowest-CPU.}
Selects the backend reporting the lowest CPU\% at the most recent poll cycle.
Falls back to round-robin until the first successful metrics poll marks
\texttt{CPUPolled = true}.

\paragraph{Intelligent (static multi-metric).}
Computes a weighted score for each backend:
\[
  \text{score}_i = w_L \cdot \hat{\ell}_i + w_C \cdot \hat{c}_i + w_K \cdot \hat{k}_i
\]
where $\hat{\ell}_i$, $\hat{c}_i$, $\hat{k}_i$ are min-max normalised
values of total observed response latency (\texttt{AvgLatencyMs}), CPU\%,
and connection count across the current backend pool,
and $(w_L, w_C, w_K) = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2)$ by default.
The backend with the \textit{lowest} score is selected. Falls back to
round-robin during cold-start. Because \texttt{AvgLatencyMs} captures
total end-to-end RTT (network $+$ compute), the algorithm naturally
penalises both distant and overloaded backends without requiring explicit
baseline subtraction.

\paragraph{Adaptive (dynamic multi-metric).}
Extends the intelligent selector with a background goroutine that recomputes
weights every 10\,s based on the variance of each metric across the backend
pool:
\[
  w_m = \frac{\text{Var}(m)}{\text{Var}(\text{latency}) +
             \text{Var}(\text{CPU}) + \text{Var}(\text{connections})}
\]
Metrics with higher cross-backend variance receive higher weight, causing the
algorithm to focus attention on the dimension that most differentiates backend
load. Weights are unchanged when total variance is zero (uniform backends).

\subsection{Cold-Start Fix}

A key implementation detail is the handling of the period between load balancer
startup and the first successful metrics poll ($\sim$5\,s). Metric-based
algorithms that default to selecting \texttt{backends[0]} during this window
route all initial traffic to a single backend, typically the weakest one.

All metric-based selectors implement a \textit{warmup round-robin} fallback:
a package-level atomic counter distributes requests evenly until latency data
(\texttt{AvgLatencyMs > 0}) or CPU poll data (\texttt{CPUPolled = true}) is
available. This eliminates the cold-start pile-on.

\subsection{Load Generator and Benchmarking Methodology}

A dedicated Go client (\texttt{cmd/client}) supports two modes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closed-loop:} $N$ concurrent workers each issue a request,
        wait for the response, then issue the next. Throughput is bounded by
        latency $\times$ concurrency.
  \item \textbf{Open-loop:} Requests are fired at a fixed target rate using a
        batch ticker (100\,ms intervals, batch size = rate/10). An in-flight
        counter with a configurable cap allows measuring the saturation point
        where offered load exceeds capacity.
\end{itemize}

The client records per-request timestamps, total RTT, compute time
(from the backend response body), network time (RTT $-$ compute), backend ID,
work type, and error status to a CSV file for offline analysis.

\subsubsection*{Closed-Loop vs.\ Open-Loop: Why Both Matter}

The two modes answer fundamentally different questions, and conflating them
leads to incorrect conclusions about system behaviour.

\textbf{Closed-loop} (the workload profile sweep) bounds concurrency.
With $N$ workers, at most $N$ requests are ever in flight simultaneously.
If the system slows down, workers block and throughput falls; the system
self-regulates. This models real browser or RPC clients that wait for a
response before sending another request. It is the right tool for comparing
algorithm \emph{quality} under a fixed load level: which algorithm produces
lower latency and better distribution when concurrency is held constant?
The tradeoff is that a closed-loop client cannot drive the system past the
point where $N$ concurrent requests saturate all backends --- it automatically
backs off.

\textbf{Open-loop} (the saturation ramp) fires requests at a fixed rate
regardless of whether prior requests have completed. If the system falls
behind, a queue of in-flight requests accumulates and latency rises without
bound. This models HTTP clients driven by external arrival processes
(e.g., user traffic, upstream services) that do not slow down because your
load balancer is struggling. Open-loop testing is the correct tool for
finding the \emph{saturation point} --- the offered-load rate at which the
system transitions from the stable regime (latency near service time) to the
unstable regime (latency grows without bound, errors appear).

The combination is essential: closed-loop reveals \emph{how well each
algorithm routes under steady load}; open-loop reveals \emph{how much load
each algorithm can sustain before breaking}. An algorithm that looks good in
closed-loop but routes traffic poorly can fail earlier than expected in
open-loop because it concentrates load on a single backend, saturating it
while others remain idle.

In our experiment, the 120-second closed-loop profiles used 5 distinct
workload configurations to probe algorithm behaviour under different
stress patterns (CPU saturation, high connection count, I/O-bound, mixed).
The open-loop ramp stepped through offered loads of 10--200\,req/s in
seven steps, each held for 120\,s to allow the system to reach steady state
before recording metrics.

\paragraph{CPU-bound workloads (cpu\_only, high\_conn, bimodal, realistic).}
Least-connections is the consistent winner across all CPU-heavy profiles. The
p99 tail latency improvement is the more compelling metric: on \texttt{cpu\_only}
it achieves 1{,}224\,ms versus 2{,}689\,ms for round-robin (55\% reduction);
on \texttt{high\_conn}, 4{,}838\,ms versus 8{,}934\,ms (46\% reduction).
Throughput gains are more modest at 10--17\% over round-robin, reflecting that
the bottleneck shifts from queueing to raw compute once tail latency is
controlled. The mechanism is straightforward: least-connections implicitly
discovers backend capacity by tracking queue depth. Backend-3 (4 vCPU)
processes requests faster, drains its connection count sooner, and therefore
attracts proportionally more new requests.

\paragraph{I/O-bound workloads.}
All algorithms converge to $\sim$350\,req/s because compute is not the
bottleneck --- the fixed 250\,ms sleep dominates every response regardless of
which backend handles it. Lowest-latency gains a marginal edge (374.7\,req/s,
p50=266\,ms) by routing all traffic exclusively to backend-2 (northcentralus,
$\approx$14\,ms RTT), the geographically closest node. Since the 250\,ms
sleep is identical on every backend, the only differentiator is network
round-trip time, and lowest-latency's pure-proximity selection wins.
This is the ideal workload for a network-proximity algorithm: compute is
constant, so minimising network RTT directly minimises total response time.

\paragraph{Intelligent and adaptive.}
Both algorithms land between round-robin and least-connections on CPU-heavy
profiles. They avoid the worst pathological routing behaviours but do not fully
exploit the capacity asymmetry that least-connections discovers through its
queue-depth signal. The adaptive algorithm recomputes weights every 10\,s,
giving approximately 12 recomputation cycles per 120\,s run, which is
sufficient for weights to converge toward the dominant metric before the
experiment window closes.

The pure-proximity lowest-latency algorithm routes \emph{all} traffic to
backend-2 (northcentralus, 14\,ms RTT) --- the definitive behaviour of a
network-proximity algorithm. This is optimal for I/O-bound workloads where
compute is constant, but on CPU-bound workloads it starves the 4-vCPU
backend-3 entirely while overloading a 2-vCPU backend. The saturation point
of 75\,req/s reflects this over-concentration; the cluster's 4-vCPU capacity
in backend-3 is completely wasted. This illustrates a fundamental limitation
of single-metric routing: optimising one dimension necessarily creates blind
spots in the others.

Lowest-CPU distributes more sensibly (18\%/46\%/36\%), correctly reducing
traffic to the heavily-loaded 1-vCPU backend-1. The improved CPU measurement
(asynchronous sampling with per-core normalisation) allows the algorithm to
observe the relative load accurately: backend-1 (taskset-pinned to 1 core)
runs at $\approx$100\% CPU, so lowest-CPU routes only 18\% of traffic there
and redirects the bulk to the faster backends. However, it still cannot exploit
the 1:2:4 capacity ratio optimally because it lacks a throughput signal.

Least-connections distributes as (29:37:35), which is closer to a uniform
split than the capacity-ideal 14:29:57, but the implicit queue-depth signal
still directs the weakest backend proportionally less work, explaining its
consistently superior tail latency.

This is the open-loop manifestation of the closed-loop observation: lowest-latency
is optimal for I/O-bound work but routes 100\% of CPU-bound traffic to a
single 2-vCPU backend, hitting its capacity wall at 75\,req/s while the
4-vCPU backend-3 sits entirely idle. Lowest-CPU saturates
at 100\,req/s where p99 reaches 9{,}339\,ms; the improved CPU measurement
correctly deprioritises the overloaded backend-1 but still cannot fully exploit
the 4-vCPU capacity of backend-3.

The adaptive algorithm shows a cold-start p99 spike (5{,}807\,ms) at
10\,req/s --- the first weight recomputation fires at $t=10$\,s and the
resulting routing shift causes a burst of requests to queue on a newly preferred
backend whose metrics have not yet stabilised. The true capacity knee is at
100\,req/s (p99 = 17{,}532\,ms), and errors begin at 150\,req/s (8.9\%).
Adaptive's saturation behaviour thus falls between intelligent (150\,req/s
clean) and lowest-latency (75\,req/s), reflecting its variance-proportional
weight adaptation: it converges toward the dominant metric signal but can
transiently route poorly during recomputation steps.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

\subsection{Lessons Learned}

\paragraph{Least-connections is remarkably effective for free.}
The single most impactful insight from this project is that tracking in-flight
request count --- a zero-overhead signal available without any polling ---
yields consistently superior p99 tail latency on CPU-bound workloads across
every profile tested, with a 46--55\% p99 reduction over round-robin. It also
achieves or exceeds round-robin throughput in all cases. The connection count
serves as an implicit capacity signal: a backend that processes requests faster
always has fewer active connections, so the algorithm implicitly discovers and
exploits compute asymmetry without needing to measure CPU or latency directly.

\paragraph{Network proximity and compute capacity pull routing in opposite directions.}
The backend layout creates a fundamental tension: backend-2 (northcentralus)
is the closest at $\approx$14\,ms RTT but has only 2 vCPUs; backend-3
(eastus2) is farther at $\approx$39\,ms but has 4 vCPUs. For I/O-bound
workloads, network proximity wins --- routing to backend-2 gives the lowest
total latency. For CPU-bound workloads, compute capacity wins --- routing to
backend-3 drains the queue faster despite the extra 25\,ms of network
overhead. Single-metric algorithms cannot navigate this tradeoff; they pick
one dimension and overfit to it.

\paragraph{Lowest-latency is the right tool for the right workload.}
The lowest-latency algorithm uses each backend's minimum observed
\texttt{/metrics} poll RTT (\texttt{BaselineRTTMs}) as a direct selection
metric --- pure network-proximity routing. It consistently routes to
backend-2 (northcentralus, $\approx$14\,ms), the geographically closest
node. On I/O-bound workloads, this is \emph{optimal}: because compute time
is fixed and identical across backends, minimising network RTT minimises
total response time, and lowest-latency achieves the highest throughput
of any algorithm on the \texttt{io\_bound} profile. On CPU-bound workloads,
this is \emph{suboptimal}: backend-2 has only 2 vCPUs, so routing
\emph{all} CPU-heavy traffic to it (100\% in the cpu\_only profile) causes it
to saturate while the 4-vCPU backend-3 sits entirely idle. The saturation
point of 75\,req/s reflects this over-concentration. The lesson is not that lowest-latency is broken ---
it is that proximity-only routing is the correct strategy if and only if
compute time is not a bottleneck.

\paragraph{Multi-metric scoring spreads load more evenly than single-metric.}
The intelligent algorithm achieves the best balance: it distributes CPU-only
traffic at 24/39/37 (b1/b2/b3), closer to the capacity-ideal 14/29/57 than
any single-metric algorithm other than least-connections. It also sustains the
highest saturation point among the metric-based algorithms (150\,req/s), just
one step below the cluster ceiling.

\paragraph{Cold-start and weight recomputation timing matter.}
The adaptive selector recomputes weights every 10\,s. Over a 120\,s experiment
window this gives approximately 12 recomputation cycles, enough for the
algorithm to converge toward the dominant metric signal rather than being
dominated by the cold-start distribution. In earlier experiments with a
30\,s recomputation cycle and a 90\,s window, only 3 recomputations fired
per run, and the initial weight shift caused a burst of requests to queue on
a newly preferred backend whose metrics had not yet stabilised --- this
manifested as an apparent saturation artefact at 10\,req/s. Aligning the
recomputation interval to a fraction of the experiment window is critical for
adaptive algorithms to produce meaningful results.

\paragraph{Open-loop clients must use context cancellation for safe drain.}
Early iterations of the load generator used a \texttt{select} between a
results channel and a \texttt{drainDone} channel to unblock in-flight goroutines
after the deadline. This has an unavoidable race: when both cases are
simultaneously ready, Go selects randomly, meaning goroutines can write to a
closed channel. The correct fix is passing a \texttt{context.WithCancel} to
\texttt{http.NewRequestWithContext}: cancelling the context aborts all in-flight
HTTP requests, \texttt{wg.Wait()} completes deterministically, and
\texttt{close(results)} is then safe.

\subsection{What Remains}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Weighted round-robin:} Assign static weights proportional to
        vCPU count. This would route 57\% of traffic to backend-3 from the
        start, requiring no metrics polling and likely matching least-connections
        on CPU-bound throughput.
  \item \textbf{Request shadowing / canary:} Send a fraction of requests to
        all backends to maintain live metric estimates even when a backend
        receives little organic traffic (the starvation problem seen with
        lowest-latency and backend-2).
  \item \textbf{Adaptive weight smoothing:} The current 10\,s recomputation
        cycle eliminates the cold-start artefact, but large variance shifts
        can cause abrupt routing changes. Adding exponential smoothing
        ($w_t = \alpha w_{\text{new}} + (1-\alpha) w_{t-1}$) would prevent
        oscillation when one metric temporarily spikes.
  \item \textbf{Power-of-two-choices:} Sample two random backends and route to
        the better one. Expected to combine the simplicity of round-robin with
        near-optimal load distribution~\cite{power-of-two}.
  \item \textbf{TLS and HTTP/2:} Current implementation is HTTP/1.1 only.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Connection to CS640 Material}

This project directly exercises the core routing concepts covered in CS640.
inteliLB is a layer-7 routing system: it maintains a global view of backend
state, computes a per-request forwarding decision, and updates its policy based
on observed network and system conditions — the same separation of concerns
as the control plane / data plane architecture studied in lecture.

\paragraph{The load balancer as a routing processor.}
The CS640 router architecture identifies the \textit{routing processor} as the
component that executes routing protocols, maintains routing tables, and computes
the forwarding table. inteliLB's main goroutine plays exactly this role at layer 7:
it runs the backend health poller (analogous to OSPF \texttt{hello} heartbeats),
maintains the backend state table (the link-state database), and computes a
forwarding decision for every incoming HTTP request.

\paragraph{ARPANET link-cost metrics and multi-metric scoring.}
The CS640 lectures trace three generations of ARPANET routing metrics.
Metric 1 (hop count) ignores load entirely — the analogue in inteliLB is
round-robin. Metric 2 (queue length, i.e.\ packets enqueued) routes toward the
shortest queue regardless of whether that queue leads to the right destination —
the analogue is \texttt{least\_connections}, which the experiments confirm
over-concentrates traffic on backend-1. Metric 3 (average delay over a sliding
window, $\text{Delay} = (D_T - A_T) + T_{\text{transmit}} + T_{\text{latency}}$)
routes by observed end-to-end delay — the analogue is \texttt{lowest\_latency}.
The ARPANET finding that no single metric suffices directly motivates
inteliLB's weighted combination:
\[
  \text{score}_i = w_L\,\hat{l}_i + w_C\,\hat{c}_i + w_K\,\hat{k}_i
\]
where latency, CPU, and connection count are normalised and blended so that
over-optimising one dimension does not degrade the others.

\paragraph{Dijkstra and centralized global-state routing.}
Link-state routing (OSPF) floods topology information to every node so each
can run Dijkstra locally over a complete global view. inteliLB follows the same
model at the application layer: the load balancer polls every backend every 5\,s
to maintain a complete, current view of the system, then scores all backends in
$O(n)$ to pick the best next hop for each request. The centralized, globally-informed
decision is what distinguishes \texttt{intelligent} and \texttt{adaptive} from
the reactive, locally-scoped heuristics (\texttt{lowest\_latency},
\texttt{lowest\_cpu}) that consider only one dimension at a time.

\paragraph{Convergence, cold start, and transient disruption.}
The CS640 treatment of link-state convergence identifies two failure modes:
(1)~a detection-delay black hole, where a node continues forwarding to a failed
neighbor until heartbeat timeouts expire; and (2)~inconsistent link-state, where
routers that have not yet received a flooded update make contradictory forwarding
decisions. Both appear in inteliLB. The cold-start window — before any backend
has been polled — is the detection-delay analogue: metric-based algorithms have
no cost information and fall back to round-robin to avoid routing into an
unknown-state backend. The 5-second poll interval is the convergence window:
during it, the load balancer's view of backend CPU and latency may be stale,
causing transient mis-routing analogous to the inconsistent link-state window
during OSPF flooding.

\paragraph{Link-state vs.\ distance-vector trade-off.}
CS640 contrasts link-state (high messaging overhead, fast convergence) with
distance-vector (low overhead, slow convergence and count-to-infinity risk).
inteliLB's polling architecture is a link-state model: the single load balancer
polls all backends directly, incurring $O(n)$ messages per interval but
converging in one round. A gossip-based, peer-to-peer health model would be the
distance-vector analogue — lower polling overhead at the cost of slower,
potentially inconsistent convergence. The current single-LB design is also
structurally vulnerable to the inconsistent-state pathology when scaled to
multiple LB instances without coordination, exactly as un-synchronized routers
produce transient forwarding loops during topology changes.

\paragraph{Adaptive recomputation and periodic flooding.}
OSPF floods link-state packets both on topology events (link failure) and
periodically (every $\sim$30 minutes) to ensure consistency. inteliLB's
adaptive algorithm mirrors this dual trigger: the 5-second poller is the
event-driven channel (detects backend CPU and latency shifts), while the
30-second weight recomputation is the periodic recalculation that adjusts
the routing policy even when no discrete event has occurred. The 30-second
interval proved too coarse in the experiments — the adaptive algorithm was
frequently caught mid-adjustment during the 120-second test windows, producing
erratic distributions (0\% to one backend in the realistic profile), consistent
with the transient disruptions that occur when periodic flooding intervals are
too long relative to the rate of topology change.

\paragraph{Saturation and queueing theory.}
The saturation ramp results (Figure~\ref{fig:sat_lat}) exhibit the classic
hockey-stick latency profile predicted by $M/M/k$ queueing theory: latency
remains near the service time until utilisation approaches 100\%, then diverges.
\texttt{intelligent} sustains clean throughput to 150\,req/s before saturation
while \texttt{round\_robin} and \texttt{lowest\_latency} collapse earlier,
because smarter routing keeps all $k$ servers below their individual saturation
points longer — directly applying the CS640 insight that load distribution
determines effective system capacity.
